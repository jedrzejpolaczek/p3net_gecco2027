\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Neural architecture search explores large, discrete spaces of candidate network designs, and assessing any single candidate against the true objective, whether by training it from scratch, through weight sharing, or by querying a benchmark built for this purpose, is the dominant cost of the search~\citep{white2023insights,liu2023survey}. Treating architecture search and hyperparameter tuning, such as the choice of learning rate or weight decay, as two separate stages, first the architecture under a fixed or default hyperparameter setting, then tuning hyperparameters for the winning architecture, is common practice~\citep{lu2019nsganet,lu2020nsganetv2}. Zela et al. show it to be unreliable on two counts: architecture and hyperparameters interact, so the best architecture under one hyperparameter setting need not be the best under another; and the common shortcut of ranking architectures after only a few training epochs correlates poorly with their ranking at the full training budget~\citep{zela2018towards}. Separating the two stages compounds this risk twice over. Searching both jointly, rather than sequentially, avoids that compounding, at the cost of a further enlarged search space on top of an already expensive one.

NSGA-Net established evolutionary search directly over network architectures, training every candidate to completion~\citep{lu2019nsganet}. NSGANetV2 showed that pairing the same evolutionary engine with a learned accuracy predictor lets most candidates be screened cheaply, reserving full evaluation for a small, promising subset; multi-objective evolutionary search itself remains the field's most common approach to multi-objective NAS, and surrogate assistance is one of several efficiency techniques built on top of that engine~\citep{lu2020nsganetv2} (Section~\ref{sec:related-work}).

NSGA-Net's own experiments document that 60--80\% of generated genotypes decode to duplicate architectures as the number of nodes grows~\citep{lu2019nsganet}. This is evidence that variables in the encoding interact in ways an operator blind to that structure can only exploit by accident, if at all. The cost of duplicated architectures, however, can be neutralised by engineering means external to the search algorithm itself: detecting and skipping genotypes already seen, applied both when building benchmarks (canonicalising isomorphic cells, as in NAS-Bench-101~\citep{ying2019nasbench101}) and on the fly during search (NSGA-Net itself, and P3Net through its own deduplication cache, Section~\ref{sec:proposed-optimizer}); this has been standard practice since 2019. This solution is not universal, however. NAS-Bench-201, a benchmark with its own, separate cell space, does not canonicalise isomorphic architectures: its own authors state they built it ``without considering isomorphism,'' and of 15,625 raw encodings, only 6,466 (${\sim}41\%$) are topologically unique, an ${\sim}59\%$ duplication rate~\citep{dong2020nasbench201}. This same, unmitigated redundancy therefore carries directly into JAHS-Bench-201~\citep{bansal2022jahsbench}, which is built on NAS-Bench-201's cell space and is one of this paper's two primary benchmarks (Section~\ref{sec:results}). What remains open is therefore not the existence of duplicates, but the narrower question of whether a dependency-aware search operator generates fewer of them at proposal time, rather than merely detecting them more cheaply after the fact.

A separate line of work targets exactly this dependency structure between genotype variables: linkage learning evolutionary algorithms such as GOMEA~\citep{thierens2011optimal} and the Parameter-less Population Pyramid (P3)~\citep{goldman2014parameterless}, developed largely independently of this NSGA-II-based NAS lineage. They learn which genotype variables interact and protect these groups during crossover, rather than treating the genotype as an unstructured encoding, in principle avoiding the kind of redundant, duplicate-generating search that NSGA-Net's own numbers document. Duplicates and variable dependencies, however, are two distinct phenomena that do not necessarily coincide. Duplicates arise at the decoding step: different genotypes can describe the exact same architecture, regardless of how the variables affect fitness. Variable dependencies are something else: which genotype variables jointly determine the objective. Such an operator learns exactly these dependencies, not duplicates. Whether it therefore also happens to generate fewer duplicates depends on whether the two phenomena coincide in practice: whether the same variable groups responsible for decoding redundancy are also the groups relevant to fitness. That is exactly what the duplication-rate diagnostic in Section~\ref{sec:results} checks.

P3Net combines two elements: a dependency-aware search engine and a relative, linkage-aware surrogate. The engine is P3, which replaces NSGA-II, so that crossover follows a linkage tree learned from the population rather than acting on individual variables. The surrogate, rather than regressing a candidate's absolute accuracy from its full encoding, predicts the change in accuracy relative to a parent, restricted to the one linkage subset that was modified, in the spirit of eLyMPuS, a variant of LyMPuS that predicts relative fitness change along a linkage structure discovered incrementally rather than assumed known in advance~\citep{przewozniczek2026lympus} (Section~\ref{sec:related-work} elaborates). Both the search engine and the surrogate are extended to the joint setting, where the genotype includes training hyperparameters such as learning rate and weight decay alongside architectural choices.

Central question and contribution: under a fixed, limited full evaluation budget, does P3's dependency aware search, paired with a linkage aware, relative surrogate, produce candidates with a better accuracy/computational-cost trade-off than NSGA-II's crowding distance selection paired with an absolute regressor surrogate, as in NSGANetV2? This is tested in the joint architecture and hyperparameter setting. Matched ablations separate the two levers, search engine and surrogate, as far as the design permits; the relative, linkage aware surrogate is only defined given a linkage tree, so it cannot itself be paired with NSGA-II, and Section~\ref{sec:results} details this limit of the ablation design. P3Net answers this question: a linkage learning evolutionary algorithm paired with a relative, linkage aware surrogate for joint architecture and hyperparameter search, on two benchmarks, JAHS-Bench-201 and NAS-HPO-Bench-II, under equal full evaluation budgets. It is evaluated via a controlled comparison against NSGANetV2, these matched ablations, and SH-EMOA and MO-BOHB, the baselines established for this exact joint setting on these same two benchmarks~\citep{guerreroviu2021bagofbaselines}. The closest prior work already combines a surrogate assisted linkage learning algorithm with network search, on an architecture only space~\citep{bartnik2026evolutionary}; Section~\ref{sec:related-work} details how P3Net's linkage aware relative surrogate, its direct comparison against NSGA-II/NSGANetV2, and its joint architecture and hyperparameter scope differ from that baseline.
