\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Neural architecture search explores large, discrete spaces of candidate network designs, and assessing any single candidate against the true objective, whether by training it from scratch, through weight sharing, or by querying a benchmark built for this purpose, is the dominant cost of the search~\citep{white2023insights,liu2023survey}. Treating architecture search and hyperparameter tuning, such as the choice of learning rate or weight decay, as two separate stages, first the architecture under a fixed or default hyperparameter setting, then tuning hyperparameters for the winning architecture, is common practice~\citep{lu2019nsganet,lu2020nsganetv2}. Zela et al. show it to be unreliable on two counts: architecture and hyperparameters interact, so the best architecture under one hyperparameter setting need not be the best under another; and the common shortcut of ranking architectures after only a few training epochs correlates poorly with their ranking at the full training budget~\citep{zela2018towards}. Separating the two stages compounds this risk twice over. Searching both jointly, rather than sequentially, avoids that compounding, at the cost of a further enlarged search space on top of an already expensive one.

NSGA-Net established evolutionary search directly over network architectures, training every candidate to completion~\citep{lu2019nsganet}. NSGANetV2~\citep{lu2020nsganetv2} showed that pairing that same evolutionary engine with a learned accuracy predictor lets most candidates be screened cheaply, reserving full evaluation for a small, promising subset -- directly answering the cost problem just raised. Multi-objective evolutionary search remains the field's most common approach to multi-objective NAS, and surrogate assistance of this kind is one of several efficiency techniques built on top of it (Section~\ref{sec:related-work}).

This same NSGA-Net~\citep{lu2019nsganet} lineage also documents a specific weakness worth naming here, because it points at exactly the kind of hidden structure a dependency-aware search engine -- the subject of this paper -- exists to exploit: as the number of nodes grows, 60--80\% of generated genotypes decode to duplicate architectures. That is evidence that variables in the encoding interact in ways an operator blind to that structure can only exploit by accident, if at all. On its own, though, this weakness is not fatal: the cost of duplicated architectures can be neutralised by engineering means external to the search algorithm itself, so the question is whether it counts as a solved problem for this paper's purposes. Detecting and skipping genotypes already seen is standard practice since 2019, applied both when building benchmarks (canonicalising isomorphic cells, as in NAS-Bench-101~\citep{ying2019nasbench101}) and on the fly during search (NSGA-Net~\citep{lu2019nsganet} itself, and P3Net through its own deduplication cache, Section~\ref{sec:proposed-optimizer}). That practice is not universal, however, and the gap matters directly to this paper: NAS-Bench-201~\citep{dong2020nasbench201} underlies one of this paper's own two primary benchmarks, JAHS-Bench-201~\citep{bansal2022jahsbench} (Section~\ref{sec:results}), and its own separate cell space is not canonicalised against isomorphism -- its authors state they built it ``without considering isomorphism,'' and of 15,625 raw encodings, only 6,466 (${\sim}41\%$) are topologically unique, an ${\sim}59\%$ duplication rate~\citep{dong2020nasbench201} that carries unmitigated directly into JAHS-Bench-201~\citep{bansal2022jahsbench}. What remains open, then, is not whether duplicates exist -- the mitigation above already covers that -- but the narrower question this paper actually asks: whether a dependency-aware search operator generates fewer of them at proposal time in the first place, rather than merely detecting them more cheaply after the fact.

A separate line of work targets exactly this dependency structure between genotype variables: linkage learning evolutionary algorithms such as GOMEA~\citep{thierens2011optimal} and the Parameter-less Population Pyramid (P3)~\citep{goldman2014parameterless}, developed largely independently of this NSGA-II-based NAS lineage. They learn which genotype variables interact and protect these groups during crossover, rather than treating the genotype as an unstructured encoding, in principle avoiding the kind of redundant, duplicate-generating search that NSGA-Net's own numbers document. Duplicates and variable dependencies, however, are two distinct phenomena that do not necessarily coincide. Duplicates arise at the decoding step: different genotypes can describe the exact same architecture, regardless of how the variables affect fitness. Variable dependencies are something else: which genotype variables jointly determine the objective. Such an operator learns exactly these dependencies, not duplicates. Whether it therefore also happens to generate fewer duplicates depends on whether the two phenomena coincide in practice: whether the same variable groups responsible for decoding redundancy are also the groups relevant to fitness. That is exactly what the duplication-rate diagnostic in Section~\ref{sec:results} checks.

P3Net combines two elements addressing these two, largely independent problems in turn: a dependency-aware search engine, answering the duplication/dependency question just raised, and a relative, linkage-aware surrogate, answering the evaluation-cost problem this paper opened with. The engine is P3~\citep{goldman2014parameterless}, which replaces NSGA-II, so that crossover follows a linkage tree learned from the population rather than acting on individual variables. The surrogate exists for the same reason NSGANetV2's own predictor does~\citep{lu2020nsganetv2}: it screens most candidates cheaply so that the expensive full evaluation is reserved for a small, promising subset. Rather than regressing a candidate's absolute accuracy from its full encoding, though, it predicts the change in accuracy relative to a parent, restricted to the one linkage subset that was modified, in the spirit of eLyMPuS, a variant of LyMPuS that predicts relative fitness change along a linkage structure discovered incrementally rather than assumed known in advance~\citep{przewozniczek2026lympus} (Section~\ref{sec:related-work} elaborates). Both the search engine and the surrogate are extended to the joint setting, where the genotype includes training hyperparameters such as learning rate and weight decay alongside architectural choices.

Central question: under a fixed, limited full evaluation budget, does P3's dependency-aware search, paired with a linkage-aware relative surrogate, produce candidates with a better accuracy/computational-cost trade-off than NSGA-II's crowding-distance selection paired with an absolute regressor surrogate, as NSGANetV2~\citep{lu2020nsganetv2} does? This work tests that question in the joint architecture-and-hyperparameter setting. Matched ablations separate the two levers, search engine and surrogate, as far as the design permits: the relative, linkage-aware surrogate is only defined given a linkage tree, so it cannot itself be paired with NSGA-II, a limit of the ablation design Section~\ref{sec:results} details further. P3Net -- a linkage learning evolutionary algorithm paired with a relative, linkage-aware surrogate for joint architecture-and-hyperparameter search -- is the method proposed to investigate this question. It is evaluated on two benchmarks, JAHS-Bench-201 and NAS-HPO-Bench-II, under equal full evaluation budgets, against NSGANetV2, the matched ablations above, and SH-EMOA and MO-BOHB, the baselines established for this exact joint setting on these same two benchmarks~\citep{guerreroviu2021bagofbaselines}. The closest prior work, Bartnik~\citep{bartnik2026evolutionary}, already combines a surrogate-assisted linkage learning algorithm with network search, but on an architecture-only space; Section~\ref{sec:related-work} details how P3Net differs from that baseline on three counts at once -- its linkage-aware relative surrogate, its direct comparison against NSGA-II/NSGANetV2, and its joint architecture-and-hyperparameter scope.

Stated plainly, and consistent with how Sections~\ref{sec:results} and~\ref{sec:conclusions} are actually written: the answer this work finds is substantially negative on the benchmark family tested. P3Net does not separate statistically from uniform random search in any of the sixteen (search space, budget) cells measured; a structural pathology in the population-management engine (Results, ``Diagnostics: population-pyramid bootstrap share'') is identified as a major contributor, diagnosed, and partially, but not fully, corrected; and the relative surrogate's own representational limitation, once isolated from that engine-level confound, shows no measurable advantage over a simpler absolute regressor either (Results, ``Diagnostics: surrogate representational capacity''; Conclusions). The contribution of this work is accordingly not a demonstrated efficiency win, but a rigorous, honestly-reported diagnosis of why the combination underperforms at this benchmark scale, the real, verified fixes attempted for each diagnosed cause, and a reasoned, literature-grounded account (Related Work; Conclusions) of the conditions -- larger, more structurally complex search spaces -- under which the same mechanism is expected to fare better, which this benchmark family does not provide.
