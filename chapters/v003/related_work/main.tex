\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related-work}

Evolutionary architecture search was pioneered by NSGA-Net, which employs NSGA-II as the search engine for convolutional network architectures, with selection based on nondominated sorting and crowding distance. Every candidate in this approach undergoes full training, and the only mechanism accelerating convergence is the exploitation of search history through a Bayesian network. Notably, the authors document substantial redundancy from genotype to phenotype, reporting that 60--80\% of generated genotypes correspond to duplicate architectures as the number of nodes grows, which indicates a nontrivial, nonlinear dependency structure hidden within the architecture encoding~\citep{lu2019nsganet}.

A year later, NSGANetV2 introduced an accuracy predictor refined online during the search, and replaced full training from scratch with fine tuning of weights inherited from a supernet, reducing the number of fully evaluated architectures to 350 (Table 2 of the original study)~\citep{lu2020nsganetv2}. This substitution trades training cost for a documented risk of rank disagreement between weight shared and independently trained accuracy~\citep{yu2020evaluatingnas}.

This scheme continues a pattern White et al. identify as the most common approach to multi-objective NAS: evolving architectures with a multi-objective evolutionary algorithm, for which NSGANetV2 itself is cited as an example~\citep{white2023insights}. Liu et al. list pairing that engine with a learned surrogate as one of several recognised techniques for reducing evaluation cost, alongside weight sharing and zero-cost proxies; neither survey singles out this combination as the field's one dominant paradigm~\citep{liu2023survey}.

Further development in this direction has moved toward predictors based on ranking rather than regression, such as RankNet in MoSegNAS~\citep{lu2022mosegnas}, which extends the NSGANetV2 lineage to semantic segmentation. Notably, this extension is not a plug-and-play reuse of NSGANetV2's own predictor: the authors motivate RankNet specifically because generic or overly simple surrogates transfer poorly to the new task, and pair it with a redesigned encoder-decoder search space and a second, latency-specific surrogate, rather than porting the classification-era surrogate and search space unchanged. A separate strand critically reassesses zero cost proxies, whose documented weakness is unreliable discrimination among top ranked architectures.

In parallel, a separate family of evolutionary algorithms has developed that explicitly models structural dependencies between genotype variables, namely GOMEA and the Parameter-less Population Pyramid (P3)~\citep{thierens2011optimal,goldman2014parameterless}. These algorithms build a linkage tree from statistical dependencies observed in the population and use it to guide crossover, protecting detected groups of dependent variables from being disrupted. P3 additionally counteracts the premature convergence typical of generational models: unlike NSGA-II, it does not discard previously found good solutions while introducing new diversity. Structurally, P3 replaces a single fixed-size population with an ordered pyramid of populations of growing size (Figure~\ref{fig:p3-pyramid}), adding a new level only once existing levels stop yielding improved solutions. This property gives the algorithm its name, parameter-less: population size is not chosen in advance. Building and promoting a single new solution up the pyramid canonically requires a real fitness evaluation at every accepted local improvement, not only at the end of the process; this detail matters directly for budget accounting under P3Net's small evaluation budgets (Section~\ref{sec:results} returns to it when discussing the surrogate-free P3 ablation).

It is worth noting that the population level, statistically learned linkage described above should not be confused with gray box optimization in the narrower sense established by Whitley et al.~\citep{whitley2016graybox}. That notion is used within the GOMEA line of work, including its real-valued extension RV-GOMEA~\citep{andreadis2024maxclique} and the joint GOMEA library~\citep{bouter2023library}: there, the optimizer is given explicit access to the subfunctions composing the objective and exploits this access to perform cheap, partial fitness reevaluations after a localized variable change. The present work instead operates in the black box regime: NAS validation accuracy cannot be decomposed into locally recomputable subfunctions, so P3 here serves purely as a search engine aware of the genotype's dependency structure.

As with NSGA-Net and NSGANetV2, the cost of full evaluation remains the central bottleneck for linkage learning evolutionary algorithms as well: each optimal mixing step proposes a partial variable change whose effect on the objective must, under this black box regime, be verified through a full fitness evaluation. Pairing this search engine with a cheap learned surrogate that screens candidates before committing to a full evaluation is therefore a natural extension.

Combining linkage learning algorithms with a learned surrogate has already been explored, though not in the NAS domain. CS-GOMEA integrates GOMEA with a convolutional neural network acting as a surrogate, evaluated on synthetic combinatorial problems (Onemax, Trap functions, NK landscapes, HIFF); the authors explicitly identify the surrogate's failure to exploit linkage tree information in its own construction as an open research direction~\citep{dushatskiy2019csgomea}. Dushatskiy et al.~\citep{dushatskiy2021novelsurrogateassistedevolutionaryalgorithm} integrate a surrogate directly with a P3 variant, the first such documented case, testing the approach on an expensive, real world partition based ensemble learning problem; notably, an SVR model outperformed a neural predictor in their experiments. The most recent work in this line, LyMPuS~\citep{przewozniczek2026lympus}, introduces a surrogate integrated directly with the linkage discovery mechanism and verifies it experimentally in combination with P3, again on classical combinatorial benchmarks such as NK landscapes, Ising Spin Glasses, and Max3Sat.

This validation domain, classical combinatorial benchmarks rather than NAS, is also where the P3/GOMEA lineage's advantage over generic search has been shown to scale specifically with problem size and structural complexity, not merely to hold at one fixed scale. Goldman and Punch report a lower order of computational complexity in evaluations for P3 than five comparison algorithms across NK landscapes, Ising spin glasses, and MAX-SAT, over problem sizes spanning roughly two orders of magnitude~\citep{goldman2015fast}. Qiao and Gallagher formalise the same pattern for GOMEA on concatenated trap functions, deriving a runtime bound of $O(m^3 2^k)$ for $m$ subfunctions of length $k$ under a truthful linkage model, whose gap over a structure-blind $(1+1)$ EA's $O(\ln(m)(mk)^k)$ widens asymptotically with both $m$ and $k$~\citep{qiao2024gomearuntime}. CS-GOMEA's own comparison shows the same pattern empirically rather than asymptotically: the Bayesian optimisation baselines it compares against, SMAC and Hyperopt, degrade past 16 variables, while CS-GOMEA continues to find the optimum at 400~\citep{dushatskiy2019csgomea}. The cell search space evaluated in the present work (Section~\ref{sec:introduction}) is far smaller than any of these, and, as discussed below, comparatively unstructured; whether the advantage this lineage shows on its traditional, larger and more deceptive validation problems is present, absent, or attenuated at this scale is accordingly an open question this comparison does not resolve on its own (Section~\ref{sec:results} returns to this when discussing what, if anything, in the present results is consistent with it).

P3Net's surrogate builds specifically on eLyMPuS, the ``empirical'' variant of LyMPuS introduced in the same work. eLyMPuS replaces LyMPuS's assumption of a known true dependency structure with a dependency structure discovered incrementally, and possibly incompletely, exactly the black-box regime P3Net operates in. Mechanistically, however, $\hat{\delta}_F$ regresses a continuous fitness difference rather than eLyMPuS's discrete better/worse/ambiguous comparison, which places its computational mechanism closer to CS-GOMEA's regression-based relative surrogate~\citep{dushatskiy2019csgomea}. The debt to eLyMPuS is therefore philosophical, not mechanistic: both are relative, linkage-aware estimators over an incompletely discovered structure, and P3Net accordingly forgoes eLyMPuS's monotonicity-conditional recovery guarantee. Section~\ref{sec:proposed-optimizer} details the adaptation.

Among applications of linkage learning algorithms directly to NAS that forgo a learned surrogate entirely, the only identified work is Tran et al., combining GOMEA with Synaptic Flow, a training free metric computed analytically that requires no learning from prior evaluation outcomes~\citep{tran2023gomeanas}. This constitutes a fundamentally different cost reduction mechanism than a learned surrogate, and, as discussed below, is distinct from Bartnik's surrogate assisted linkage learning applied to NAS~\citep{bartnik2026evolutionary}.

Independently of linkage learning approaches, a narrower body of work has addressed the joint optimisation of network architecture and training hyperparameters, rather than treating hyperparameter tuning as a separate step following architecture search~\citep{zela2018towards}. Standardised benchmarks for this setting have since been introduced, namely JAHS-Bench-201~\citep{bansal2022jahsbench}, a surrogate benchmark over a joint search space combining categorical and continuous variables, and NAS-HPO-Bench-II~\citep{hirose2021nashpobenchii}, a fixed grid lookup table over combinations of architecture and hyperparameters. Such benchmarks exist for the same reason NAS-Bench-101 does: precomputing or approximating the objective once lets many search runs be compared on identical, reproducible ground truth, rather than repeating live training for every candidate and every compared method. This trade-off, between an exact, enumerable ground truth and a learned, continuous approximation, is discussed further below~\citep{ying2019nasbench101}.

A systematic comparison of solvers on this setting evaluates standard baselines such as SH-EMOA (a multi objective evolutionary algorithm built on SMS-EMOA's dominated-hypervolume selection~\citep{beume2007smsemoa}), Multi-Objective Bayesian Optimization Hyperband (MO-BOHB, combining BOHB's own Bayesian-optimisation-guided Hyperband~\citep{falkner2018bohb} with a random-weight Tchebycheff scalarisation of the objectives), and random search; a fourth baseline used in the present comparison, the Tree-structured Parzen Estimator (TPE)~\citep{bergstra2011tpe}, is not itself examined by this line of work but is adopted here on the same footing. None of the methods compared in this line of work is a linkage learning evolutionary algorithm. P3Net adopts SH-EMOA and MO-BOHB as additional baselines directly from this comparison (Section~\ref{sec:results}): unlike NSGANetV2, which is imported from architecture only NAS and extended here to the joint setting, SH-EMOA and MO-BOHB were established specifically for the joint architecture-and-hyperparameter setting itself~\citep{guerreroviu2021bagofbaselines}, not for a benchmark shared with P3Net's own evaluation -- that comparison's own evaluation instead uses a custom search space (Oxford-Flowers, Fashion-MNIST at $16\times16$) that predates both JAHS-Bench-201 and NAS-HPO-Bench-II by several months, so the setting, not a specific shared benchmark, is what carries over here.

Across this joint NAS+HPO literature, the hyperparameter half of the search space is consistently the training procedure, not the network's structural capacity. JAHS-Bench-201~\citep{bansal2022jahsbench} searches learning rate, weight decay, activation function, and data augmentation as hyperparameters; the number of cells and the channel width are instead treated as a separate fidelity axis, alongside training epochs and input resolution, distinct from both the architecture and hyperparameter dimensions. NAS-HPO-Bench-II~\citep{hirose2021nashpobenchii} varies only learning rate and batch size as hyperparameters, on top of a fixed cell topology search space; training duration is handled through a separate surrogate rather than as a searched variable.

Zela et al. likewise jointly optimise the categorical meta-parameters of a multi-branch ResNet architecture: the number of residual blocks and branches per block, and the per-block widening factor. These are searched together with seven continuous training hyperparameters: initial learning rate, batch size, momentum, L2 weight decay, CutOut length, MixUp $\alpha$, and ShakeDrop death rate. Training epochs serve only as the resource dimension of Hyperband here, not as a decision variable.

P3Net follows the same convention (Section~\ref{sec:introduction}). Bartnik~\citep{bartnik2026evolutionary} sidesteps this design question entirely, since her genotype has no hyperparameter dimension at all: training procedure is fixed by the NAS-Bench-201 lookup table, and her own discussion acknowledges that the resulting conclusions describe that closed search space rather than NAS in general, a limitation that directly motivates evaluating on genuinely joint benchmarks such as those above.

The joint NAS+HPO literature surveyed above also differs in the source of its ground truth. Zela et al. optimise the joint setting through BOHB on real training runs. NAS-HPO-Bench-II~\citep{hirose2021nashpobenchii} answers every query through a fixed lookup table of measured values, while JAHS-Bench-201~\citep{bansal2022jahsbench} answers through a learned surrogate; neither trains anything at evaluation time. Benchmarks of the first kind provide a fixed, exhaustively enumerable lookup table, over which an exact oracle Pareto front is therefore computable; we call these \emph{Category 1}. Benchmarks of the second kind answer every query through a learned, continuous surrogate over the search space and admit no enumerable oracle front; we call these \emph{Category 2}. In this terminology, NAS-HPO-Bench-II is Category 1 and JAHS-Bench-201 is Category 2.

Classifying NAS-HPO-Bench-II as Category 1 assumes that P3Net queries only its exhaustively tabulated range: up to 12 training epochs, with three recorded seeds per entry. The benchmark itself also exposes a separate, learned surrogate (a GIN network combined with an MLP) that extrapolates results to 200 epochs. If that extrapolation were what constituted the full-evaluation level $r_K$, it would undermine both this classification and the exact-oracle-front status. It does not: P3Net's full-evaluation level $r_K$ for this benchmark is fixed at the tabulated range only, and the 200-epoch surrogate extrapolation is never queried by the search loop. The Category~1 classification and the exact-oracle-front status therefore both hold.

P3Net's primary benchmarks are these same two, chosen for the reasons already established in this line of work and in the Category 1 NAS literature more broadly: a deterministic objective, a computable oracle front, and a bounded evaluation budget.

JAHS-Bench-201's own surrogate should not be conflated with the learned surrogate that P3Net, CS-GOMEA~\citep{dushatskiy2019csgomea}, Dushatskiy et al.~\citep{dushatskiy2021novelsurrogateassistedevolutionaryalgorithm}, and LyMPuS~\citep{przewozniczek2026lympus} build during the search itself. JAHS-Bench-201's surrogate stands in for the objective function once, offline, before any search begins. The surrogate used by these linkage learning methods works differently: it is fitted incrementally during the search, from candidates already evaluated, purely to decide which further candidates are worth a full query. These are two independent design choices at two different levels; P3Net's contribution concerns only the second, the search-time surrogate, layered on top of whichever substrate answers the full evaluation.

Among works pairing such a search-time predictor with a linkage learning algorithm, the closest to the present work is Bartnik. She adapts SA-P3-GOMEA, a surrogate assisted linkage learning algorithm, to multi objective NAS on NAS-Bench-201, optimising validation accuracy against measured GPU energy consumption. The surrogate used there is an absolute regressor over the flat genotype (deterministic or probabilistic variants of SVR, MLP, random forest, or gradient boosting). The compared baselines are MO-LS and surrogate free MO-P3-GOMEA, not NSGA-II or NSGANetV2. The search space is the closed NAS-Bench-201 benchmark restricted to architecture only, with no training hyperparameters included in the genotype.

Stepping back from Bartnik's specific choices, the broader survey literature confirms this algorithm family's marginal position: three independent systematic surveys of the evolutionary NAS literature~\citep{liu2023survey, white2023insights, ozcelik2026survey} do not list GOMEA, P3, or linkage learning more broadly as a distinct method category, indicating that this algorithm family remains outside the mainstream of the field. The combination of a linkage learning EA with a learned surrogate has already been empirically validated, and, as described above, Bartnik's work is the first to extend it to NAS. Four aspects remain jointly unaddressed, however: a linkage aware, relative surrogate rather than an absolute regressor over the flat genotype; NSGA-II/NSGANetV2 as a directly compared baseline; the joint search over architecture and hyperparameters, to which, to our knowledge, no linkage learning evolutionary algorithm has previously been applied; and the effect of linkage awareness on the genotype duplication rate in a graph-cell search space. Even Bartnik, the only prior work in this line operating on such a space (NAS-Bench-201, hence susceptible to the same isomorphism as JAHS-Bench-201, discussed in Section~\ref{sec:introduction}), does not report this rate as a separate diagnostic.

A further limitation shared by both benchmarks used in the present work, distinct from the single-dataset scope discussed in Section~\ref{sec:results}, is that JAHS-Bench-201 and NAS-HPO-Bench-II instantiate the same NAS-Bench-201-style cell search space~\citep{dong2020nasbench201} and the same small-scale image classification task. This is not a property specific to the present comparison but a documented feature of the wider NAS benchmarking literature. Yang et al.\ show that search-space design, more than the search algorithm itself, accounts for much of a method's reported accuracy, and that method rankings measured on one dataset do not reliably hold on another~\citep{yang2020nasfrustratinglyhard}. Mehta et al.\ reach the same conclusion at larger scale, running 25 search-space/dataset combinations and finding that conclusions, and even search-algorithm hyperparameters, tuned on one NAS benchmark frequently fail to transfer to another~\citep{mehta2022nasbenchsuite}. Lopes et al.\ examine the NAS-Bench-201 cell space directly and find its operation pool redundant and its performance distribution skewed toward already-good architectures, so that most randomly sampled architectures already perform close to the best achievable one~\citep{lopes2023nasbenchdesign}; on this reading, the search space shared by both benchmarks evaluated here sits toward the easy, low-variance end of the range NAS benchmarks span, a property any result obtained on it should be read against. Tu et al.\ and Duan et al.\ document the same generalisation gap along the task-domain axis specifically: NAS methods validated on image classification transfer inconsistently, in some cases catastrophically, to other tasks such as PDE solving, protein folding, or dense pixel-level prediction, and the benchmark suites purpose-built to test this, NAS-Bench-360 and TransNAS-Bench-101 respectively, both report exactly this instability~\citep{tu2022nasbench360,duan2021transnasbench101}. None of this is specific to linkage learning evolutionary algorithms; it is a property of the NAS benchmarking literature as a whole, and the present comparison inherits it along with everything else built on this search-space family (Conclusions).
